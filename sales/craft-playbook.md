# The craft: interviewing, positioning, offer, selling

Companion to [first-customers-playbook.md](first-customers-playbook.md). Procedures to run, not
theory. Sources are the practitioners' own words where marked; paywalled material is flagged
rather than paraphrased from summaries.

## 0. Do this first, it costs nothing and nobody else can

**Jane's feature-request board is login-gated.** Their own guide: *"This board is only accessible
by users with an active staff profile associated with a Jane account."*

Aidan has a clinic login. Search it for **front desk schedule, staff roster, room utilization,
shift swap, coverage, time off approval** and record the upvote counts.

That is direct demand evidence, from the incumbent's own customers, that no competitor can
reach. It is the single highest-value hour available and it requires no outreach, no product
change and no permission.

## 1. The Jane facts, from Jane's own documentation

These are the ammunition. Never say "Jane can't do X." Say "Jane's docs say X." You are quoting
the vendor they trust, not attacking it.

**Verified, quotable:**

- **Front desk staff cannot be scheduled at all.** *"Jane's scheduling features are designed
  around patient appointments, so there isn't a way to add administrative or front desk staff
  shifts to the Schedule tab at this time."* And separately: administrative staff *"will not
  display on the Schedule."*
- **Rooms do not prevent double-booking.** *"if multiple practitioners are assigned to the same
  rooms at the same time, **you can accidentally overbook the rooms!**"*
- **No room utilization report.** Only an estimate from Hours Booked, exported to Excel.
- **No hourly wage rates.** *"Jane doesn't store hourly wage rates."* The Compensation Report is
  commission-only; other models must be calculated outside Jane.
- **Rooms and Resources are Thrive-tier only.** A prospect on Practice who says "we already do
  that" is describing a feature they do not pay for. Ask which plan they are on.
- **No open API, and no plans for one.** So **never claim Tempo "syncs with Jane."** It would be
  false. The gated developer platform exposes appointments read-only, staff, and locations, with
  **no endpoints for shifts, availability, rooms or resources**.
- **None of Jane's 18 partner integrations is a staff-scheduling or workforce tool.** Open lane.
  Emailing partnerships@jane.app costs nothing.

**Concede this one honestly:** Jane's *Resources* genuinely do prevent equipment double-booking
on Thrive. Saying so on a call buys the credibility for the six points above.

**The structural conclusion.** Across every practice-management vendor checked, rooms and
resources are an *appointment attribute*. Not one of them rosters humans. The category models
"when is this provider bookable," never "who is working Saturday and who covers when Sarah calls
in sick." That is a category boundary, not a feature gap, which is why "we already have that in
our EMR" is a genuine misunderstanding rather than a brush-off.

## 2. Interviewing

### The rule that makes colleague interviews safe

Fitzpatrick, twice: *"the fact that they're friends won't bias your data, so long as you ask
good questions"* and *"Now that you're armed with The Mom Test, they won't be able to lie to you
even though they know you."*

**The bias is not caused by the relationship. It is caused by mentioning your idea.** A
colleague can only perform helpfulness if there is something to be helpful about.

His posture flip: *"don't go into these discussions looking for customers. It creates a needy
vibe and forfeits the position of power. Instead, go in search of industry and customer
advisors."*

### The three rules

1. Talk about their life instead of your idea
2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future
3. Talk less and listen more

### Questions to read aloud

- **"Talk me through the last time that happened."**
- **"What else have you tried?"** — *"If they haven't looked for ways of solving it already,
  they're not going to look for (or buy) yours."*
- **"How are you dealing with it now?"** — this gives you the price anchor
- **"What are the implications of that?"** — separates I-will-pay-to-fix-that from
  that's-annoying-but-I-can-live-with-it
- **"Why do you bother?"**
- **"Where does the money come from?"**
- **"Who else should I talk to?"** — end every conversation with this
- **"Is there anything else I should have asked?"**

Never: "Do you think it's a good idea?" · "Would you buy a product that did X?" · "How much
would you pay?" All three invite a lie.

### The recovery script, memorise it

> **"Whoops, I just slipped into pitch mode. I'm really sorry about that, I get excited about
> these things. Can we jump back to what you were just saying? You were telling me that..."**

### The self-audit

You are going through the motions if: you talked more than they did · they complimented you ·
you told them your idea and don't know what happens next · you have no notes · you weren't
scared of any question you asked.

*"There's more reliable information in a 'meh' than a 'Wow!'"*

### How many

- Fitzpatrick: 3-5 if the segment is tight; more than 10 still scattered means the segment is
  too fuzzy
- Moesta: **10**, and his reason is unusual — *"If you conduct over ten interviews, the intent
  gets lost."* Signal degradation, not saturation
- Guest, Bunce & Johnson (2006), peer-reviewed: **12 interviews captured 92% of themes** in a
  homogeneous sample; base themes by 6
- **Blank's go/no-go, the number that matters:** *"five of the first 12 customers you call on
  saying, 'I need this right now, even if it's still buggy.'"*

### Two deliverables Blank demands and we have never produced

1. **A day-in-the-life of the customer, before and after the product**
2. **An org chart of users versus buyers**

Neither is a slide. Both are the exit criteria for customer discovery.

## 3. Positioning

### The story that is exactly ours

Dunford, on selling a database against Oracle:

> *"'We don't need a database, they explained, we have Oracle'. For them, 'database' meant a lot
> more than just an ability to do a fast query, **our unique value was sidelined by everything
> else they trusted Oracle to do.**"*
>
> *"**Positioning ourselves in the database market highlighted what we couldn't do, put us into
> direct competition with a very strong competitor, and made our critical value seem like an
> unimportant add-on.**"*

Swap database for scheduling and Oracle for Jane. *"We don't need a scheduler, we have Jane"* is
the same sentence.

**So: the competitive alternative is the spreadsheet sitting next to Jane and the group text
thread, not Jane itself.** Positioning against Jane accepts the frame where we are a worse Jane.

Her own words on the status quo: *"Often they are using things like spreadsheets and documents,
or manual processes, or making do with the limited functionality included with their current
'monolith' platform... You need to position yourself as clearly superior to the status quo."*

### Against a well-liked leader

She concedes the leader's strength first: *"The accepted market leader in a space has a massive
competitive advantage because they are the low-risk choice."*

Then names the only two attack surfaces: *"meeting the needs of specialized segments and quickly
responding to market changes."* Jane is not slow, so **specialization is the only one available.**

Use **Style 2, the sub-market frame**: *"you can frame your product around something that
prospects already understand, without having to take the category leader head-on."*

### Stop rewriting the positioning

> *"If your product hasn't launched yet, what you're actually creating is a **positioning
> thesis**, not true positioning."*
>
> *"there is a risk of over-tightening the positioning too soon... **keep your positioning loose
> pre-launch. Launch the product, see where the market pulls you, then tighten up.**"*

Her cadence is a check-in every **six months**, plus emergencies. Four rewrites in three months
with zero customer input is not iteration, it is the same guess in new clothes.

Her diagnostic for whether positioning is even the problem requires happy existing customers and
recorded sales calls. We have neither, so by her own test we cannot currently diagnose a
positioning problem at all.

And the limit: *"**If the product truly is undifferentiated, then positioning will not help
you.**"*

## 4. The offer

### The verdict on "I'll build one process for you free"

All three pricing practitioners reject it, for different reasons.

**Hormozi** is not anti-free. His protocol is *"make the first five free"* — but standardized,
capped at five, with a named exit (*"Once people start referring, start charging"*, then 80% off
the next five, then 60%). An open-ended bespoke build violates it before it starts: no cap, no
standardization, no trigger.

His diagnostic, which reframes the whole problem:

> *"**If you struggle to give your stuff away for free, it means either people don't want it
> (dream outcome), they don't believe you (perceived likelihood of achievement) or the hidden
> costs (time, effort and sacrifice) are too high. In short, your 'free' stuff is too
> expensive.**"*

**Stark** replaced free discovery with a productized, named, priced diagnostic. His own is a
**$1,295 Roadmapping Session**. The man who teaches value pricing puts a price on his own
diagnosis.

**Enns** has the line that is a complete drop-in replacement:

> *"a first step should be just that, **a first step and not a sample twenty-fifth step**. By
> this I mean start at the beginning, which is almost always your diagnosis of the problem... To
> jump right to project work... you'll do it **without the client's full involvement, without
> fully applying your methods, and without appropriate compensation.**"*
>
> *"Offer an opt-out point... You can further sweeten the pot by adding a money-back guarantee
> for the first phase."*

And on refusing to guarantee while pitching free: *"nothing is more ludicrous than a firm that
will pitch for free but bristles and balks at the idea of offering a guarantee."*

### The value equation

Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)

> *"When comparing two products that satisfy the same desire, **the value from the dream outcomes
> will cancel out**. It will be the other three variables that drive the difference."*

"A schedule that works" is also what the spreadsheet promises, so it cancels. **We compete on
time delay and on effort and sacrifice.** Hormozi: *"the older I get, the more I have shifted my
focus to 'the hard stuff', decreasing the bottom side of the equation."*

### Where they genuinely disagree, do not blend

- **Options.** Enns: always three, anchor high with option C, one page, they hear the price
  before they see it. Hormozi: **one** offer, stacked until saying no feels stupid. These are
  structurally incompatible. Pick one.
- **Guarantees.** Hormozi: aggressive and stacked, but warns guarantee-motivated buyers make bad
  clients. Enns: guarantee **the decision, not the result**, and require payment in advance.
- **Anchoring.** Enns anchors high on price. Hormozi never anchors on price, he raises perceived
  value until the price looks small.

### The one thing all three converge on

Replace the free build with a **branded, named, one-page, paid first-phase diagnostic**, with an
opt-out point and a money-back guarantee on phase one.

## 5. Selling with no track record

**Kazanjy's substitute for logos is domain authority:** *"Expertness in the vertical in which you
are selling is an absolute requirement... ideally **even more expert than the prospects to whom
you are selling. This expertness will make you fearless.**"*

Running a real 75-staff clinic schedule is that credential. It is stronger than three logos.

His warning, which cuts against using colleagues as leads: *"**Targeting based on relationship
rather than need is ineffective and a waste of time.**"* Colleagues are for learning. Strangers
with the pain are for selling.

His discovery transition, read aloud:

> *"I like to start with some questions so I have a better idea of how your team goes about its
> day to day, and so I can do a better job of focusing on things you'll get more value out of."*

On price: *"**Just state the pricing and the rationale that backs it, shut the hell up, and see
what the reaction is.**"*

And do not over-qualify: *"the problem with strict 'letter of the law' qualification when you're
selling an early-stage product is that oftentimes, you're having a conversation with someone who
had no idea that this solution existed."*

### Handling "we already have that in our EMR"

1. **Validate.** *"Sounds like Jane is checking all the boxes on the patient side. Honestly it's
   a great product, that's not a fight I'd pick."*
2. **Label, then pause.** *"It sounds like the patient booking side is genuinely handled."*
3. **Poke the bear.** The best single question available:
   > **"Just out of curiosity, when someone calls in sick on a Saturday, what happens? Walk me
   > through the last time that happened."**

   A clinic owner cannot answer that generically, and the answer is almost always a group text
   and a spreadsheet. Alternatives: *"where does the front desk schedule actually live?"* and
   *"how do you know how many hours your treatment rooms sat empty last month?"*
4. **Run the pain funnel.** How long has that been a problem? · What have you tried? · Did any of
   it work? · How much has it cost you? · Have you given up trying to deal with it?
5. **Ask about the room.** *"How on board are the people not on this call?"*
6. **Reframe with the vendor's own words**, as information rather than argument.

Josh Braun's governing principle: *"**Sort me, don't sell me.**"* And: *"The best way to persuade
is to allow other people to persuade themselves."*

## The sequence

1. **Today.** Search Jane's Canny board. Record the top upvoted requests on staff scheduling,
   rooms and coverage.
2. **This week.** Ten casual conversations inside the clinic. Never mention Tempo. Produce
   Blank's two artifacts: day-in-the-life before and after, and the org chart of users vs buyers.
3. **Weeks 2-4.** Ten switch interviews with external clinics. Half who recently bought any
   scheduling tool, half who abandoned one, all within 90 days.
4. **Then** write the positioning thesis, keep it loose, position against the spreadsheet.
5. **Replace the offer** with the paid one-page diagnostic.
6. **First calls:** up-front contract, Kazanjy's transition line, pain funnel, poke the bear,
   state the price and stop talking.

**The gate at step 3 is Blank's: five of the first twelve saying "I need this right now, even if
it's still buggy." If that does not happen, the problem was never positioning.**
