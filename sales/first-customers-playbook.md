# Getting customer #1: what worked for people who actually did it

From first-person accounts by Patrick McKenzie (Appointment Reminder), Joel Friedlaender
(Cliniko), Alison Taylor and Trevor Johnston (Jane App), Arvid Kahl, Rob Walling, Jason Cohen,
Amy Hoy, Peldi Guilizzoni, Justin Jackson, Nathan Barry, Daniel Ruscigno.

## How customer #1 was actually found

| Rank | Mechanism | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Insider position: own workplace, partner's practice, co-founder's employer** | Jane, Cliniko, FeedbackPanda, Transistor, ClinicSense (5) |
| 2 | A hand-worked list of named humans, never more than ~100 | Cohen 50 to 30, Walling 17 to 11, Barry 100 to 5-7 (3) |
| 3 | Existing community standing | Hoy, Barry, Jackson (3) |
| 4 | Conference floor, in person | ClinicSense, Jane (2) |
| **0** | **Cold canvassing of clinics** | **Cliniko: documented zero** |

Three things that produced nothing, all first-person:

- Cliniko: "he walked door-to-door and clinic-to-clinic and asked people if they were
  interested, but **none of those people were convinced**."
- Cohen, launching to a 30,000-subscriber blog: "**I got two sign-ups. Two.**"
- Peldi, on 3-4 Hacker News front pages: "they rarely correspond to big spikes in sales."

**3,400 cold emails for 12 replies and 0 meetings was not an execution failure. It reproduced a
documented zero.**

## What insider access buys

Cliniko's founder on having a practitioner beside him: "It was a huge bonus... **It was
essential.**"

1. **Customer zero under real load.** Canopy ran Jane for a year before anyone else touched it.
2. **Engineer the leak.** Jane's entire first-12 came from a credit line in a booking-page
   footer. Trevor Johnston: "other clinics saw her online booking, and so they were contacting
   my company, **because we had our little name at the bottom in the footer**... maybe 10 or 12.
   This was our entire market research." Patient booking is naturally public. Staff scheduling
   is not, so the equivalent has to be built on purpose.
3. **Shadow someone for a day.** McKenzie's stated biggest regret: "find one person within like
   6 degrees of social separation from me and just ask to shadow her for a day and then I would
   have figured that out like really freaking quick."
4. **Find the structural trap before pricing.** McKenzie never asked whether hairstylists were
   employees or chair-renters: "it is like literally no skin off their nose if somebody does not
   come in... not caring about the core value proposition makes it rather difficult to sell."
   The clinic analogue: which practitioners are contractors, and do they bear any cost from a
   bad schedule?
5. **Practitioners on staff who also work elsewhere** are the highest-leverage referral vector
   in the building.

Why this market spreads by referral: practitioners "are forced to collaborate" and "**have to
attend annual conferences to get their education credits, which puts them all in a room
together.**" 80-85% of Jane's new business is referral, three accounts per demo, no sales force.

## Who the buyer is

McKenzie's "Office Manager Milly": "the office manager who would otherwise be doing the calls
herself... earns about $20k to $50k a year, is generally an employee rather than owner... and
**she has independent authority to make the purchasing decision.**"

Why the adjacent segment could not be sold at all: "many hair salons have no one accountable for
making sure people actually come to appointments, but Milly is accountable to the business
owner, this is one of her core tasks."

**Accountability, not economics, makes the sale.** Find who gets in trouble when the schedule
breaks. If nobody does, that clinic never buys at any price.

- Top churn reason: "We never really convinced the whole team to use it." Barry's counter, free
  done-for-you migration, cut churn from ~5.5% to 1.5%.
- They do not switch for features. Cliniko's public wedge at 14 customers was honesty about a
  competitor's fake free trial.
- Healthcare should be priced higher: privacy law means they "absolutely have to tell us" what
  they are, "we use this as a pricing segmentation factor."
- **No solo tier.** Kahl killed his $5 plan over chargebacks and support load. Barry refused
  $5-10 outright. A solo has no schedule problem.
- Anchors: Cliniko unchanged since 2011, $95/mo at 2-5 practitioners, $295 at 13-25, $395 at
  26-200. Jane $74-79/mo.

## The procedure

**0. Stop the cold email.** The one channel with a documented zero here.

**1. Answer McKenzie's question in writing: "Will the job buy this thing?"** Name who is
accountable when the schedule breaks. Do they hold authority to about $200/mo? How many of the
75 staff are contractors rather than employees? What does the clinic already buy without asking
anyone's permission, because that is the budget ceiling.

**2. Shadow the scheduler for half a day.** Record Amy Hoy's four columns: Pain, Jargon,
Worldview, Recommendations. Cohen: "Never ask what to build. This question does not work." Then
his yes-then-no test: "ask them to describe, in painstaking detail, exactly how they will use
the product in their daily life."

**3. Engineer the leak.** Attribution on every artifact a non-employee sees: exported schedules,
cover requests, calendar invites to locums, payroll exports, the printed room board. If nothing
Tempo produces ever leaves the building, that is a bigger gap than any feature.

**4. Build a list of 40, not 3,400.** Staff who also work elsewhere; clinic managers known
personally; vendors who sell into clinics but not scheduling ("ask them who they do not sell to,
and ask for referrals"); clinics yours refers patients to. Close every conversation with
Walling's line: "Hey, who else do you know who I can talk to?"

**5. Run five conversations on Cohen's script.** He got 40 of 50 to agree and only 1 asked to be
paid:

> "I will pay any fee you feel is fair for an hour of your time. I am not asking for a donation;
> I am genuinely interested in your expertise and opinion."

Write hypotheses in column A of a sheet, one open question each in column B. Cohen found half of
his were wrong. **Quote a real price mid-conversation and stop talking.** One group is shocked
it costs so much, the other says it cannot be any good that cheap. Both are data, and neither is
available if the number is never said. Then Walling: "I am less interested in your opinion and I
am more interested in hearing would you pay for this." Then take money. Barry had 10 of 10 say
they would preorder and 1 who did.

**6. Offer the thing that does not scale:** build the clinic's first two weeks of schedule
personally, on site, free. Nobody else selling into this market can.

## Scoreboard

Stop counting emails sent. Count conversations held (5 this week, 40 by day 90), times a price
was said out loud, dollars collected before any new code (1 by day 30), and paying clinics.
Cliniko had **14 paying one month after launch**. Jane reached **500 in 21 months**, about
24/month, starting in Metro Vancouver.

## Two warnings

**Peldi's rule**, which McKenzie broke and calls his central mistake: "Is what you want to spend
the next five years of your life on optimizing the scheduling at dentist offices?" He shipped
anyway and took six years to reach about $11k/mo. The difference here is insider access, which
is also the difference between his six years and Jane's 21 months.

**Cohen's 2023 recant**, which limits everything above: "validation **is not validation**...
What validation can do is _disprove_ an idea." The product already exists. The job is
conversations and collected dollars, not code.
