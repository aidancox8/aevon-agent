# The Schedule Audit

**CA$900. One clinic. Two weeks. Money back if you don't find it useful.**

This replaces "name one job your team does by hand and I'll build a working version of it, free."

---

## Why the free build had to go

Three practitioners, three separate reasons, same verdict.

**Hormozi** is not against free. His protocol is "make the first five free" — standardized, capped
at five, with a named trigger to start charging. An open-ended bespoke build fails all three
conditions. And his diagnostic explains the silence better than any copy critique:

> *"If you struggle to give your stuff away for free, it means either people don't want it, they
> don't believe you, or the hidden costs are too high. **In short, your 'free' stuff is too
> expensive.**"*

A free build costs the clinic staff time, data access, change management, and the risk of
depending on a solo vendor. That is not free. It is expensive and unpriced.

**Enns** names the structural error exactly:

> *"a first step should be just that, **a first step and not a sample twenty-fifth step**... To
> jump right to project work that is based on a bunch of assumptions... you'll have to do some
> form of strategic work to be able to deliver a tactical solution, but you'll do it **without
> the client's full involvement, without fully applying your methods, and without appropriate
> compensation.**"*

**Stark** simply prices his: a $1,295 Roadmapping Session. The person who teaches value pricing
productized his own diagnosis rather than giving it away.

---

## The offer

**A written Schedule Audit of one clinic, delivered in two weeks.**

1. **How the schedule actually gets built today.** Who does it, on what day, in what tool, and
   how long it takes. Timed, not estimated.
2. **Where it breaks.** Sick calls, room clashes, a practitioner changing availability, cover
   chasing. With the count from the last month, not an impression.
3. **What you already own and are not using.** Per feature, citing the vendor's own
   documentation. Most Jane clinics on Thrive pay for Rooms and Shift Management and use
   neither.
4. **What your software genuinely cannot do**, with the vendor's words rather than mine. Jane's
   docs say administrative and front desk staff cannot be added to the Schedule tab, that you
   can accidentally overbook rooms, and that there is no room utilization report.
5. **A fix list split three ways:** do it yourself this week, worth paying someone for, and no
   configuration solves this.

Item 5 is what makes it worth paying for rather than a sales call in disguise. If everything in
the findings conveniently points at buying Tempo, they will notice, and they should.

---

## Why CA$900

Below the CA$1,500 clinic assessment because this is narrower: one process, not the whole
operation. Above nothing, because the commitment is most of the value.

Reference points a clinic already understands: Jane Thrive is CA$99/mo plus per-practitioner
fees, so a 12-practitioner clinic is near CA$1,200/mo. Canadian SMB bookkeeping runs CA$300-800
a month. Stark charges $1,295 for a 90-minute call plus a document. CA$900 reads as most of one
month of an ordinary service, which is a number a practice manager can approve without a
committee.

**Do not discount it and do not waive it.** Enns:

> *"nothing is more ludicrous than a firm that will pitch for free but bristles and balks at the
> idea of offering a guarantee."*

---

## The guarantee

**"If you don't find the audit useful, don't pay for it."**

Enns is specific that you guarantee the *decision*, not the *result*: you cannot promise their
schedule improves, because that depends on them. You can promise they will not regret hiring
you. His two conditions apply:

- **Paid up front.** The commitment is the point. A guarantee after payment is risk reversal; a
  free build is just free.
- **They agree to work the way you describe.** Three hours of interview time with the owner, the
  practice manager, and whoever actually builds the schedule each week. That last person is the
  one who knows.

---

## Scope discipline

- **One clinic, one process, two weeks.** Not a retainer, not open-ended.
- **No implementation inside the fee.** Findings only. Implementation is quoted separately and
  most of it should be Tempo.
- **No production patient data.** Roster, schedule and room information only. There is no
  clinical reason to touch anything else and every reason not to.
- **Three hours of interviews, capped.**

---

## How to say it on a call

After the pain funnel, not before. Kazanjy: *"Just state the pricing and the rationale that backs
it, shut the hell up, and see what the reaction is."*

> "Here's what I'd suggest. Before anyone talks about software, I'd do a Schedule Audit. Two
> weeks, I sit with whoever builds your schedule, I time what it actually takes, I count where
> it breaks, and I write it up. You get a fix list split into things you can do yourself this
> week, things worth paying for, and things no software solves. It's nine hundred dollars, and
> if you don't find it useful you don't pay for it."

Then stop talking.

**If they ask whether they can skip the audit and just buy the software:** that is a good sign,
and the answer is still no. Enns: *"No doctor ever diagnoses without prescribing."* You cannot
scope or price the build without it, and doing the diagnosis invisibly and unpaid is exactly the
trap the free offer set.

---

## Kill criteria

Three paid audits that produce no Tempo conversation means this is consulting, not distribution,
and consulting is a job. Stop and reconsider.

Conversely, if audits sell easily and Tempo never comes up, that is also information: the
diagnosis may be the business and the software may be the distraction.
